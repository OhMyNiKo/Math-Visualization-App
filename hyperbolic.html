Hyperbolic Functions and the Catenary

View A builds cosh, sinh and tanh out of two exponentials: drag the probe x✱ along the plot and slide t to see the point (cosh t, sinh t) ride the unit hyperbola with a sector of area t/2 behind it, exactly as (cos θ, sin θ) rides the circle. View B hangs a real chain between two supports you can drag, lays the "obvious" parabola over it, and magnifies the difference until you cannot un-see it.

The defining equations \(\displaystyle \cosh x = \frac{e^x+e^{-x}}{2},\ \sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{2} \) · \(\displaystyle \cosh^2 x-\sinh^2 x = 1 \) · \(\displaystyle y = a\cosh\frac{x}{a} \)

On the unit circle the sector from (1,0) to (cos θ, sin θ) has area θ/2. On the unit hyperbola x² − y² = 1 the sector from (1,0) to (cosh t, sinh t) has area t/2. That is what makes t "the hyperbolic angle", even though no rotation is involved.

a is the horizontal tension divided by the weight per unit length; a small a means a slack, heavy chain (deep sag), a large a a tight one. The parabola is drawn through the same two supports with the same lowest point, so it is the best parabola one could hope for, and it still misses.

The misconception this kills

"A hanging chain is a parabola." Galileo said so, and it is wrong: the tension in a chain grows along its length because it carries more chain, and that makes the curve y = a cosh(x/a), a catenary. A parabola is what you get when the load is uniform per unit of horizontal distance (a suspension-bridge deck), not per unit of chain.

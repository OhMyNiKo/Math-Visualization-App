Matrices as Linear Transformations

A \(2\times2\) matrix is a recipe for where the two basis arrows go; the whole plane follows. Set the four entries or pick a preset, watch the grid deform from the identity to \(M\), drag the point \(p\) to see its image \(Mp\), and read the determinant off the parallelogram as a signed area.

The defining equation \(\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},\ \det M = ad-bc \)

Controls

Amber arrow: image of e₁ = (a, c). Blue arrow: image of e₂ = (b, d). Dashed violet lines: eigenvector directions, when the eigenvalues are real.

The misconception this kills

"The determinant is the size of the matrix." It is not: \(\det M\) is the factor by which \(M\) scales every area (the unit square becomes a parallelogram of area \(|\det M|\)), and its sign says whether orientation survives (+) or the plane is flipped over (−). Big entries can still give \(\det M = 0\), which squashes the whole plane onto a line. Right now \(\det M = \) ?.

Mathematics: the grid, unit square and point are mapped exactly by \( M(t) = (1-t)I + tM \) (entrywise interpolation, so the intermediate frames are themselves linear maps); \( \det M = ad-bc \); eigenvalues from \( \lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+\det M = 0 \), eigenvectors from \( (M-\lambda I)v=0 \), drawn only when the discriminant is non-negative.

The Chain Rule as Gears

A composite \( y=h(g(f(x))) \) is a train of gears: turn the \( x \) gear at unit speed and each next gear turns faster or slower by exactly the local derivative of its layer, backwards when that derivative is negative. Slide \( x \), watch the gear speeds change, and read the three small graphs below whose tangent slopes are the three ratios being multiplied.

The defining equation \(\displaystyle \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{dv}\,\dfrac{dv}{du}\,\dfrac{du}{dx} \) · with \(\displaystyle u=f(x),\ v=g(u),\ y=h(v) \): the ratios multiply along the train

The misconception this kills

"The derivative of a composite is the sum of the layer derivatives." No: gears in a train multiply their ratios, they never add them. A layer with slope 0 stops the whole train no matter how steep the others are, and a layer with slope −1 reverses everything after it. Mathematics: each layer parsed to an expression tree and evaluated exactly; layer derivatives and the composite derivative by central differences with \( h=10^{-4} \) (error \( O(h^2) \)); gear angular speeds are the exact products of the local derivatives, integrated frame by frame, with the whole train's speed scaled so the fastest gear stays readable.

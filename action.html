The Least Action Principle

A ball must get from A to B in a fixed time, through gravity. The path nature picks is drawn from the start; it is the one with the least action S. Grab any handle and bend the path however you like: S is recomputed live, and it can only go up.

The defining equation \(\displaystyle S[y]=\int_0^T\left(\tfrac12 m\dot y^{2}-mgy\right)dt \)  · nature: \(\displaystyle \delta S = 0 \)

The misconception this kills

The ball does not take the straight line, and nature is not "trying paths". The true motion is the stationary point of S: bend the curve anywhere, in any direction, and ΔS = ? only grows. The parabola is the one shape that trades kinetic against potential energy so perfectly that every tiny wiggle cancels to first order. Quantum mechanics keeps score the same way, with phases.

Physics: fixed endpoints and fixed total time T = 1.2 (m = 1); trial path y(t) is a Catmull–Rom curve through nine handles, sampled at 400 points; S = Σ(½ẏ² − gy)Δt. The analytic optimum y∗(t) = y₀ + v₀t − ½gt² with v₀ = (y₁−y₀+½gT²)/T is integrated with the same rule, so ΔS ≥ 0 up to rounding. Faint arrows: the uniform gravity field.
